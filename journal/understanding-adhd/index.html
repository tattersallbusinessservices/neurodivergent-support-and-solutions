Understanding ADHD: A Lifespan Overview

Estimated reading time: 15–20 minutes

Estimated reading time: 15--20 minutes

Introduction

Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is one of the most recognised forms of being neurodivergent.

Despite this, ADHD remains one of the most misunderstood neurodevelopmental conditions.

Many people think of ADHD as simply meaning that someone is easily distracted, unable to concentrate, overly active or impulsive. While these experiences can occur, ADHD is much more complex.

ADHD affects the way people regulate:

  • attention.
  • motivation.
  • impulses.
  • activity levels.
  • emotions.
  • planning.
  • organisation.
  • everyday self-management.

ADHD can influence how someone learns, works, manages daily tasks and experiences relationships.

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition, meaning that the differences associated with ADHD are present from early development.

However, ADHD is not always recognised during childhood.

Some people receive a diagnosis when they are young, while others are not identified until adolescence or adulthood. Many adults who receive an ADHD diagnosis describe a sense of finally understanding difficulties that have existed throughout their lives.

For many people, receiving an ADHD diagnosis provides something incredibly important:

An explanation.

It can help explain experiences that may previously have been interpreted as:

  • laziness.
  • lack of effort.
  • poor organisation.
  • carelessness.
  • not reaching their potential.

A diagnosis does not change who someone is.

It provides a framework for understanding themselves more accurately.

ADHD is one example of being neurodivergent. If you are unfamiliar with these terms, our article "Understanding Neurodiversity: What Do Neurodiversity, Neurodivergent and Neurotypical Mean?" explains the terminology used throughout the Knowledge Hub.

Key Takeaways

  • ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition affecting attention regulation, impulse control, activity levels and executive functioning.
  • The differences associated with ADHD are present from early development, although diagnosis may happen during childhood, adolescence or adulthood.
  • ADHD affects people of all ages, genders, intelligence levels and backgrounds.
  • ADHD is not caused by poor parenting, laziness or lack of intelligence.
  • Many difficulties associated with ADHD arise from the interaction between a person's neurological profile and the environment around them.
  • Being neurodivergent does not mean being deficient, but ADHD can create genuine challenges requiring appropriate support.
  • Understanding ADHD can replace self-blame with self-understanding.
  • A diagnosis is an explanation, not an excuse.

What Is ADHD?

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition that affects the way the brain regulates attention, behaviour and self-management.

The name itself can sometimes create confusion.

The term "attention deficit" suggests that someone with ADHD simply lacks attention.

This is not accurate.

Many people with ADHD can concentrate extremely well when they are:

  • engaged in what they are doing.
  • emotionally invested in the activity.
  • sufficiently motivated.
  • experiencing an appropriate level of challenge.

The difficulty is often not an inability to pay attention.

The difficulty is regulating attention consistently.

Someone with ADHD may struggle to:

  • begin a task that feels overwhelming.
  • maintain concentration during repetitive activities.
  • switch attention appropriately.
  • filter distractions.
  • organise competing demands.

However, that same person may become deeply absorbed in an activity that captures their attention or provides strong motivation.

This is sometimes described as an interest-based pattern of attention.

The more useful question is not:

"Can this person pay attention?"

but:

"Can this person regulate their attention effectively across different situations?"

ADHD and Executive Function

One of the most helpful ways to understand ADHD is through the concept of executive functioning.

Executive functions are the mental processes that help us manage ourselves and achieve goals.

They include:

  • planning.
  • organising.
  • prioritising.
  • starting tasks.
  • remembering information.
  • managing time.
  • regulating emotions.
  • controlling impulses.
  • adapting when circumstances change.

They can be thought of as the brain's management system.

People with ADHD may understand exactly what they need to do but struggle to consistently translate that understanding into action.

For example:

A person with ADHD may know that an important task needs completing.

They may understand why it matters.

They may genuinely want to complete it.

Yet they may still find it extremely difficult to start.

This is not the same as not caring.

It reflects differences in how attention, motivation, reward and executive control interact.

A useful phrase is:

Knowing what to do and being able to consistently do it are not always the same thing.

What Causes ADHD?

The causes of ADHD are complex.

Current evidence suggests that ADHD arises from a combination of genetic influences and differences in neurodevelopment.

There is no single cause.

There is also no evidence that ADHD is caused by parenting style, lack of discipline or personal failure.

Genetics

ADHD has a strong genetic component.

Children with ADHD are more likely to have a parent or close relative with ADHD, although the way ADHD appears can vary significantly between family members.

ADHD is not caused by one single gene.

Instead, many genetic factors contribute to differences in brain development and function.

Brain Development

Research has identified differences in the development and functioning of brain networks involved in:

  • attention regulation.
  • motivation.
  • planning.
  • impulse control.
  • emotional regulation.

These differences should not be interpreted as meaning that the ADHD brain is "damaged".

A more accurate description is that the brain develops and functions differently.

Being different does not mean being defective.

Environmental Factors

Certain early-life factors may increase the likelihood of ADHD characteristics developing.

These may include:

  • premature birth.
  • pregnancy-related factors.
  • early developmental influences.

However, these are risk factors rather than simple explanations.

ADHD is a complex condition and cannot usually be attributed to one individual event.

Most importantly:

ADHD is not caused by poor parenting.

What ADHD Is Not

There are many misconceptions about ADHD.

ADHD is not caused by:

  • laziness.
  • lack of intelligence.
  • poor parenting.
  • lack of discipline.
  • not trying hard enough.
  • eating too much sugar.

Many people with ADHD work extremely hard.

In fact, some spend years using enormous effort to compensate for difficulties that others do not see.

This can lead to:

  • exhaustion.
  • frustration.
  • anxiety.
  • reduced confidence.

The difficulty is often not a lack of effort.

It is that the strategies being used require far more effort than they should.

ADHD in Childhood

ADHD is often first recognised during childhood.

Children with ADHD may experience difficulties with:

  • maintaining attention in lessons.
  • completing schoolwork.
  • remembering instructions.
  • organising belongings.
  • waiting their turn.
  • controlling impulses.
  • managing emotions.

However, children with ADHD are not simply children who "do not listen" or "do not try".

A child may understand expectations perfectly but still struggle to consistently meet them because of differences in:

  • impulse control.
  • working memory.
  • emotional regulation.
  • attention regulation.

This distinction is crucial.

A child who cannot consistently perform a task is not necessarily a child who does not understand the task.

Parenting a Child With ADHD

Parenting any child can be challenging.

Parenting a child with ADHD can involve additional challenges because many everyday expectations rely heavily on skills that may be more difficult for children with ADHD.

One of the most important things for parents to understand is that children with ADHD are not simply choosing their behaviour.

A child who repeatedly forgets instructions, loses belongings or struggles to complete tasks may not be refusing.

They may be experiencing genuine difficulties with executive functioning.

Understanding ADHD does not mean removing expectations or avoiding boundaries.

Children need:

  • structure.
  • consistency.
  • guidance.
  • support.

However, approaches that work well for many neurotypical children may not always work as effectively for children who are neurodivergent.

This does not mean expectations should be lowered.

It means approaches may need to be adapted.

Effective support often involves:

  • understanding the child's individual profile.
  • adapting communication.
  • making expectations clear.
  • reducing unnecessary barriers.
  • supporting emotional regulation.
  • recognising strengths as well as difficulties.

Unfortunately, parents of neurodivergent children are sometimes given advice that assumes a child is choosing their behaviour rather than experiencing neurological differences.

This can leave parents feeling blamed or misunderstood.

A more helpful question is:

"What does this child need in order to succeed?"

rather than:

"Why will this child not behave like everyone else?"

ADHD During Adolescence

Adolescence often brings increased challenges.

As young people become more independent, they are expected to manage:

  • homework.
  • revision.
  • deadlines.
  • organisation.
  • appointments.
  • relationships.
  • decision-making.

These demands rely heavily on executive functioning.

A young person who coped with significant adult support may struggle when those external supports reduce.

This does not mean they are less capable.

It may mean they need different strategies and support.

ADHD in Adults

ADHD does not disappear when someone becomes an adult.

However, the way ADHD appears may change.

Adults with ADHD may experience difficulties with:

  • organisation.
  • managing time.
  • maintaining routines.
  • completing tasks.
  • finances.
  • household responsibilities.
  • emotional regulation.
  • balancing competing demands.

Many adults are diagnosed after years of wondering why everyday tasks seem disproportionately difficult despite significant effort.

For some people, diagnosis provides an explanation for experiences that have been present since early development but never understood.

ADHD and Differences in Presentation

Historically, ADHD was most commonly recognised in children whose behaviour was highly visible. These children were often described as being constantly on the go, impulsive or disruptive in the classroom. As a result, early research and clinical descriptions were heavily influenced by these presentations.

Our understanding of ADHD has developed considerably since then.

We now recognise that ADHD can present in many different ways. While some people experience obvious hyperactivity and impulsivity, others experience difficulties that are much less visible to those around them.

These may include:

  • becoming easily distracted by their own thoughts.
  • difficulty organising work or managing time.
  • feeling overwhelmed by everyday demands.
  • struggling to start tasks despite genuinely wanting to complete them.
  • using enormous amounts of effort to appear organised or "keep up".

Some people also develop highly effective coping strategies that mask their difficulties, meaning that the underlying ADHD may not be recognised for many years.

Understanding these differences is important because ADHD should not be defined by stereotypes. It is the overall pattern of characteristics, their persistence from early development and their impact on everyday life that are important.

ADHD in Women and Girls

Historically, ADHD has been under-recognised in many girls and women.

This is partly because the earliest descriptions of ADHD were based largely on children---predominantly boys---whose difficulties were obvious within school environments. The assessment tools and clinical understanding that developed from this work naturally reflected those presentations.

As research has expanded, it has become clear that ADHD can present differently across individuals.

Some girls and women may be more likely to experience:

  • internal distractibility.
  • excessive effort to stay organised.
  • emotional overwhelm.
  • masking or compensating for difficulties.
  • exhaustion from constantly trying to meet expectations.

These difficulties may attract less attention than obvious hyperactivity, meaning that some people are not recognised until adolescence or adulthood.

This does not mean that ADHD is fundamentally different in males and females.

Rather, it reminds us that neurodevelopmental conditions can present in a wide variety of ways and that assessment should focus on understanding the individual rather than relying on stereotypes.

ADHD and Emotional Regulation

Although emotional regulation is not included within the core diagnostic criteria for ADHD, many people with ADHD describe emotions as being more difficult to regulate.

This may include:

  • becoming frustrated more quickly.
  • experiencing emotions intensely.
  • finding it difficult to move on after disappointment.
  • feeling overwhelmed by situations that others appear to manage more easily.

Everyone experiences strong emotions.

The difference for some people with ADHD is that regulating those emotions may require considerably more effort.

Recognising this can be helpful for individuals, families, schools and employers, as it allows strategies to be developed that support emotional regulation rather than interpreting every emotional response as deliberate behaviour.

ADHD, Mental Health and Self-Understanding

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition, not a mental health disorder.

However, research consistently shows that people with ADHD experience higher rates of mental health difficulties than the general population.

Understanding why this happens is important.

It would be an oversimplification to say that ADHD itself causes anxiety or depression.

A more helpful way of thinking about this is to consider the cumulative impact of living with unsupported ADHD, particularly when it has not been recognised.

Many people spend years:

  • trying harder than those around them simply to achieve the same outcomes.
  • receiving criticism despite making genuine efforts.
  • feeling that they continually fall short of expectations.
  • believing that they are lazy, careless or not trying hard enough.

Over time, these repeated experiences can have a profound effect on self-esteem and psychological wellbeing.

The mental health difficulties that develop may therefore arise not simply because someone is neurodivergent, but because they have spent years living without understanding, appropriate support or environments that allow them to thrive.

For many people, diagnosis becomes an important turning point.

Not because it changes who they are.

But because it changes how they understand themselves.

Instead of asking:

"What is wrong with me?"

people often begin asking:

"What has made life difficult, and what can I do differently?"

That shift from self-blame to self-understanding can itself be an important step towards improving wellbeing.

The same principles apply to many forms of being neurodivergent, including autism and other neurodevelopmental differences.

From Diagnosis to Understanding

Receiving an ADHD diagnosis can feel like reaching the end of a long journey.

For many people it brings relief, validation and an explanation for experiences that may have existed since early childhood.

In reality, however, diagnosis is not the end of the process.

It is the beginning.

A diagnosis tells us that an individual's experiences are consistent with ADHD.

It does not tell us precisely how ADHD affects that particular person.

No two people with ADHD are identical.

Although two individuals may both meet the diagnostic criteria, the challenges they experience in everyday life may be very different.

One person may struggle primarily with organisation and time management.

Another may find emotional regulation has the greatest impact.

Someone else may experience sensory sensitivities, sleep difficulties or problems coping with change.

Others may have developed highly effective strategies in some areas while continuing to struggle in others.

For this reason, a good assessment should go beyond assigning a diagnosis.

It should aim to develop an individual formulation---a personalised understanding of how ADHD affects that person's life.

A formulation asks questions such as:

  • Which aspects of ADHD have the greatest impact on this person's everyday life?
  • Which situations or environments make those difficulties worse?
  • Which environments allow them to function at their best?
  • What strengths can be built upon?
  • What practical changes are most likely to improve their quality of life?

These questions move beyond the label itself.

The goal is not simply to say:

"You have ADHD."

The goal is to understand:

"How does ADHD affect you, and what can we do about it?"

The answers will be different for every individual.

For some people, relatively small changes to their environment may make a substantial difference.

Others may benefit from coaching, psychological support, medication, educational support or workplace adjustments.

Many people benefit from a combination of approaches.

The ultimate aim is not simply to provide a diagnosis.

It is to improve everyday functioning, reduce unnecessary barriers and help people build a life that makes the best use of their strengths while reducing the impact of their difficulties.

Diagnosis provides the explanation.

Individual understanding helps identify the way forward.

Strengths and Challenges

ADHD is often discussed only in terms of difficulties.

Equally, it is sometimes portrayed as a "superpower".

Neither description captures the whole picture.

Many people with ADHD describe strengths associated with the way they think, including:

  • creativity.
  • curiosity.
  • enthusiasm.
  • seeing connections between ideas.
  • approaching problems from different perspectives.
  • the ability to become deeply engaged with areas that genuinely interest them.

These strengths are real.

So too are the challenges.

The same characteristic may be an advantage in one situation and a disadvantage in another.

Rather than viewing ADHD as either entirely positive or entirely negative, it is often more helpful to understand it as a different pattern of neurological development whose impact depends greatly on the interaction between the individual and their environment.

Assessment and Support

Support should always be individualised.

There is no single intervention that is right for everyone.

Effective support may include:

Understanding

Learning about ADHD often provides the foundation for everything that follows.

Understanding why particular difficulties occur can reduce self-blame and help people develop realistic, effective strategies.

Environmental Changes

Many difficulties associated with ADHD can be reduced by adapting environments rather than expecting individuals to continually adapt themselves.

Examples may include:

  • using external reminders.
  • creating predictable routines.
  • reducing unnecessary distractions.
  • breaking larger tasks into manageable steps.
  • providing clear expectations.

Developing Skills

Many people benefit from developing practical strategies for:

  • planning.
  • organisation.
  • time management.
  • prioritisation.
  • emotional regulation.

Medication

Medication can be very helpful for some people with ADHD and forms an important part of treatment for many individuals.

However, medication is rarely the whole answer.

It is most effective when combined with understanding, practical strategies and environments that support the individual rather than working against them.

Living Well With ADHD

Living well with ADHD is not about trying to become someone else.

It is about understanding how your brain works, recognising both strengths and challenges, and creating environments that allow you to function at your best.

For many people, the most important change after diagnosis is not that life suddenly becomes easier.

It is that life finally begins to make sense.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can adults develop ADHD?

No. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition, meaning the neurological differences are present from early development, even if they are not recognised until later in life.

Can intelligent people have ADHD?

Absolutely. ADHD occurs across the full range of intellectual ability. Intelligence and ADHD are entirely separate concepts.

Does ADHD mean someone is lazy?

No. Many people with ADHD expend enormous effort simply to achieve tasks that others may find relatively straightforward. The difficulty is not a lack of effort, but differences in attention regulation, executive functioning and motivation.

Can ADHD be cured?

ADHD is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition. However, understanding, appropriate support, practical strategies, environmental adjustments and, where appropriate, medication can significantly improve quality of life.

Clinical Perspective

One of the greatest benefits of diagnosis is not the label itself.

It is the opportunity to replace years of self-blame with self-understanding.

In my experience, the most valuable question is rarely:

"Does this person have ADHD?"

The more important question is:

"How does ADHD affect this individual, and what changes are most likely to help them live well?"

Diagnosis is an important milestone, but it is only the beginning.

The real goal is to understand the person, identify the barriers they face, build on their strengths and develop practical strategies that improve everyday life.

A diagnosis is an explanation, not an excuse.

It provides the foundation upon which understanding, support and positive change can be built.
