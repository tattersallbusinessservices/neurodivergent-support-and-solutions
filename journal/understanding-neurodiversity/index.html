Understanding Neurodiversity

Estimated reading time: 10–15 minutes

Introduction

The words neurodiversity, neurodivergent and neurotypical are becoming increasingly common.

You may hear people describe themselves as neurodivergent, discuss neurodiversity in schools or workplaces, or refer to conditions such as ADHD and autism as forms of neurodevelopmental difference.

However, these terms are often misunderstood.

Some people believe neurodiversity means that everyone has ADHD or autism to some degree. Others worry that talking about neurodiversity removes the importance of diagnosis or ignores the genuine challenges many people experience.

Neither interpretation is accurate.

A more helpful understanding begins with a simple idea:

Human brains naturally vary.

Just as humans vary in height, personality, interests and physical characteristics, we also vary in how our brains develop and function.

This natural variation is called neurodiversity.

Some people have neurological profiles that differ significantly from what is typical. These people may be described as neurodivergent.

Understanding these concepts provides the foundation for understanding ADHD, autism and other neurodevelopmental conditions.

Key Takeaways

  • Neurodiversity describes the variation of all human brains.
  • Neurodivergent describes an individual whose neurological development differs significantly from what is typical.
  • Neurotypical describes an individual whose neurological development broadly follows expected patterns.
  • These terms describe difference; they do not describe worth, intelligence or ability.
  • Being neurodivergent is not something that needs to be fixed.
  • Diagnosis provides an explanation for an individual's experiences and can help people access appropriate support.

What Is Neurodiversity?

Neurodiversity describes the natural variation in human brains and neurological development across the whole population.

Every person has a unique neurological profile.

People vary in areas including:

  • attention.
  • memory.
  • learning.
  • communication.
  • sensory processing.
  • emotional regulation.
  • problem-solving.

No two brains are identical.

A useful comparison is biodiversity.

Biodiversity describes the variety of living organisms within an ecosystem.

Neurodiversity describes the variety of human minds within a population.

Everyone is included within neurodiversity.

Understanding Human Variation

It is tempting to imagine that there is a simple dividing line between "normal" brains and "different" brains.

The reality is much more complex.

Human characteristics exist across a range.

For example:

  • height varies.
  • personality varies.
  • intelligence varies.
  • sensory preferences vary.
  • attention regulation varies.

Neurological characteristics vary in the same way.

The important point is not one isolated difference.

It is the overall pattern.

What Does Neurodivergent Mean?

Neurodivergent describes an individual whose pattern of neurological development differs significantly from what is considered typical.

Being neurodivergent may include people with conditions such as:

  • ADHD.
  • autism.
  • dyslexia.
  • developmental coordination disorder.
  • Tourette syndrome.
  • other neurodevelopmental differences.

Being neurodivergent does not describe someone's value, intelligence or potential.

It describes differences in how a person's brain develops and functions.

Two people who are both neurodivergent can have very different experiences.

A diagnosis describes a pattern.

It does not describe the whole person.

What Does Neurotypical Mean?

Neurotypical describes people whose neurological development broadly follows expected patterns.

It does not mean:

  • perfect.
  • superior.
  • more intelligent.
  • more capable.

Neurotypical people are also individuals with their own strengths and challenges.

The term simply describes a pattern of neurological development.

Why These Terms Matter

Language influences how we understand people.

Historically, many differences were viewed only through the lens of deficit.

A person who struggled with attention might have been described as lazy.

A person who found social situations difficult might have been described as rude.

A person overwhelmed by sensory input might have been described as difficult.

Understanding neurodiversity allows us to ask a different question:

Not:

"Why can't this person behave like everyone else?"

But:

"What does this person need in order to thrive?"

Diagnosis: Explanation, Not Excuse

Neurodiversity does not replace diagnosis.

Diagnosis remains important because it can help identify when a pattern of characteristics has a significant impact on someone's life and when support may be beneficial.

Diagnostic systems such as DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 provide frameworks to help clinicians recognise these patterns.

However, diagnosis involves professional judgement.

A diagnosis is not a simple test result.

It represents a clinician's opinion that an individual's experiences and characteristics are consistent with recognised diagnostic criteria.

Like all areas of medicine, this process is not infallible.

Clinicians are highly trained, but they are human.

Good assessment requires careful history-taking, consideration of alternative explanations and thoughtful interpretation.

Different, Not Deficient

Being neurodivergent is not something that needs to be fixed.

However, this does not mean that neurodivergent people do not experience real difficulties.

Many neurodivergent people experience challenges in areas such as:

  • education.
  • employment.
  • relationships.
  • sensory environments.
  • organisation.
  • mental wellbeing.

At the same time, some neurodivergent people identify strengths associated with their way of thinking, learning or experiencing the world.

The reality is not that being neurodivergent is simply a disadvantage or simply a gift.

It is a difference that interacts with the person and their environment.

A Diagnosis Is an Explanation, Not an Excuse

Many people seek assessment after years of struggling.

They may have spent years believing they were:

  • lazy.
  • disorganised.
  • too sensitive.
  • lacking motivation.

A diagnosis does not create these difficulties.

It provides an explanation for experiences that were already present.

Understanding allows people to develop strategies that work with their brains rather than constantly against them.

Doctor's Perspective

Understanding neurodiversity is not about deciding whether someone is "normal" or "different".

Every person is different.

The purpose of understanding neurodiversity is not to remove challenges or deny disability.

It is to understand people more accurately.

For many people, diagnosis provides something incredibly valuable:

An explanation.

Replacing years of self-blame with self-understanding can be a profound step towards better wellbeing.

Understanding allows people to work with their brains rather than constantly struggling against them.
